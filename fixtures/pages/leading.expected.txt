The ferry left the harbour an hour before dawn, with the lamps still lit along the quay and the gulls asleep on the posts.
Nobody on deck said much at first.
The engine settled into a low and steady drum that you felt more in your feet than heard, and the town slid away behind us one window at a time.
By the time the sun was up we had passed the last of the islands, and the water turned from grey to a green so clear that you could follow a rope down into it for several metres.
A boy at the rail counted the jellyfish aloud until his mother told him the number was big enough.
The captain came down to drink his coffee with the passengers and told us the crossing would take four hours if the wind held.
It did not hold.
Around ten the sky to the west went the colour of slate, and the first drops came sideways across the deck.
We crowded into the cabin, where the windows fogged at once and someone opened a tin of biscuits for everyone.
The boy fell asleep against a stranger's coat, and the stranger let him stay there until we reached the far shore.

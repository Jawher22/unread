Field notes from a small allotment
The plot sits at the far end of the row, past the water tap and the shed with the green door.
It gets sun from late morning until the light drops behind the poplars.
Nobody else wanted it, because the soil there holds water like a sponge.
The first spring went mostly to digging.
Every spadeful came up heavy and grey, and the worms seemed to be the only ones enjoying it.
By May there were four raised beds, a compost bay made from old pallets, and a path of wood chips that squelched underfoot.
Beans were the first real success.
They climbed the canes faster than anyone could pick them, and by August the neighbours had stopped accepting bags of them.
A bean glut teaches you to cook in new ways, or at least to freeze things quickly.
Tomatoes were a different story.
The plants grew tall and leafy, but the fruit split after every heavy rain.
A wet summer leaves you with more chutney than you can give away.
The compost bay turned out to be the most interesting part of the garden.
It is warm in the middle even on cold mornings, and it smells of earth rather than rot when the mix is right.
Too much grass makes it slimy, and too much cardboard makes it stall.
Keeping notes changed how the plot was run.
Each visit got one line in a notebook: the date, the weather, what was sown and what was eaten.
After a year, the notebook showed patterns that memory had smoothed over.
For example, the slugs arrived every year in the same week of April.
The first frost came later than the seed packets promised.
And the courgettes always produced their best fruit while everyone was away on holiday.
Water was the hardest thing to plan.
The tap at the end of the row runs slowly, and a full can takes almost two minutes to fill.
Mulching the beds with straw cut the watering in half during the dry weeks.
Some crops never earned their space.
Cauliflowers sulked for months and then bolted in a single warm afternoon.
Sweetcorn did well, but the badgers found it the night before it was ready.
Herbs, on the other hand, asked for almost nothing.
A clump of thyme by the path survived drought, frost and being stepped on.
The mint had to be kept in a sunken bucket, or it would have taken over the whole bed.
The second winter was quieter.
The beds were covered with cardboard and manure, and the plot was left alone to rest.
Visits became short walks to check the shed roof and to turn the compost once a fortnight.
By the third spring, the soil had changed colour.
It was darker and lighter to dig, and it crumbled instead of sticking to the spade.
Years of leaves, peelings and coffee grounds had finally done their work.
The real lesson of the plot is patience.
Most of what matters happens slowly, underground, where you cannot watch it.
The best thing a gardener can do is keep showing up and keep writing it down.
